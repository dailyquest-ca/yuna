## 1 · Why the B-arm does not run, and this does

WO-A6 §5's B-arm tranches the **bi-monthly** clock. Measured against ruling 4 it should not run,
and the arithmetic is not close: the bi-monthly phase mean is **20.58%** — below SPMO — and
tranching collects a mean rather than a maximum. Its ceiling is roughly the ETF. It stays built,
tested and unrun.

The **weekly** clock is where a 30% number already exists. All five phases are stored:

**Three of the five carry a Sharpe above SPMO's 0.987.** The mean is pulled under it by one phase,
`s5_p3`, which is 14.1 points below the best. This document has previously called the weekly clock
"leverage, not edge" on the strength of that 0.970 mean. That reading is too flat: three phases are
edge and one is not, and the arm's real problem is that **you get exactly one of them, and which
one is decided by the day of the week you happened to start on.**

That is the same date-luck the whole programme has been chasing, and tranching is the instrument
for it — the one built for WO-A6 §5 and pointed at the wrong clock.

## 2 · Construction

`every_sessions=1, tranches=5`. Every session is a rebalance date; `turn = ord % 5`, so tranche *k*
rebalances on sessions congruent to *k* mod 5. **Five weekly books on five different weekly phases,
held simultaneously.** Each owns `n/5` slots and funds its own fifth of NAV, so per-name weight
matches the single-tranche book of the same N.

N moves **8 → 10** so the five tranches divide evenly at two names each. That is why the controls
exist: `w10_p0…p4` are the five weekly phases *at N=10*, so the tranched cell is priced against its
own phase mean. Comparing a ten-name tranched book against the stored eight-name phases would move
two axes and prove nothing.

Six cells: `w10_p0, w10_p1, w10_p2, w10_p3, w10_p4, w10_t5`.

## 3 · Predictions, committed before the results

The thesis is that **tranching converts a lottery into its own expected value.** If it is right:

1. **`w10_t5` CAGR lands within ±3 points of the mean of the five `w10_p*` controls.** This is the
   central claim and the one most likely to fail. Materially *below* means tranching leaks —
   cross-tranche name rotation, a name sold by one tranche and re-bought by another, is real and
   costs spread. Materially *above* is not a win to celebrate: it would mean a rebalancing bonus I
   did not predict, and the first response is to look for a defect, not to bank it.
2. **`w10_t5` Sharpe ≥ the control-mean Sharpe.** This is the actual reason to do it. The five
   phases hold overlapping books offset by days, so they are highly but not perfectly correlated;
   a blend of imperfectly correlated series has lower volatility than the average of its parts. If
   the blended Sharpe does *not* beat the component average, the phases are effectively the same
   series and tranching buys nothing but complexity.
3. **Turnover stays in the controls' range**, ~250–310 names/yr. A tranched book trades a fifth as
   many names per date on five times as many dates. If it comes in near 5× the controls, names are
   rotating between tranches and the construction is churning rather than diversifying.

**What I do not predict:** that this beats A6 risk-adjusted. The weekly clock takes a −51%
bootstrap drawdown against A6's −36.6%. Ruling 1 accepts drawdown; it does not make it free, and
the comparison belongs in the decision line rather than being assumed away here.

## 4 · Pass bars and kills

- **Meets ruling 4** if `w10_t5` CAGR ≥ **30%** — Zak's stated target, phase-independent by
  construction.
- **Beats the ETF risk-adjusted** if Sharpe > **0.989** (SPMO on the identical window). Three of
  the five phases already do; the question is whether the blend does.
- **Kill — tranching leaks:** `w10_t5` more than 3 points below the control mean. Then the weekly
  arm is a lottery you cannot convert, and the honest options are to play one phase and accept the
  draw, or to leave it.
- **Kill — churn:** turnover > 400 names/yr. Ruling 2 relaxed the flag; it did not remove it.
- **Not a bar:** the deflated Sharpe. Ruling 3 retired it. It is still computed, still recorded,
  and these six cells will lower it for every arm in the ledger — including A6's. That is the
  correct behaviour of the statistic and the accepted cost of ruling 4.

### Scored against §3's predictions, as committed

**1 · "within ±3 points of the control mean" — PASSES, decisively.** 32.13% against 32.82% is
**−0.69 points**, and the bootstrap medians are closer still: 32.71% against 32.63%, **+0.08**.
Staggering the refresh collects the phase mean. The lottery converts.

**2 · "Sharpe ≥ the control-mean Sharpe" — FAILS.** 0.9306 against 0.9465, **−0.016**. §3 called
this "the actual reason to do it," so the failure has to be read as written: **the five phases are
effectively one series.** They hold the same names offset by days, and blending them buys
essentially no volatility reduction. The one place a benefit showed up is realized drawdown —
−40.5% against the control mean's −44.8%, shallower than four of the five phases — but the
bootstrap drawdown is flat (−49.6% against −49.2%), which says that 4.3 points is a sample artifact
and not a structural gain. **Tranching removed the phase risk. It did not reduce the volatility.**

**3 · "turnover stays in the controls' range" — MISSES.** 340.7 names/yr against a control range of
295.6–303.3, a **+13% premium**, with costs tracking it exactly ($247,663 against $219,089, +13%).
This is the cross-tranche rotation §3 named as the risk: a name dropped by one tranche and picked
up by another, which a single book would simply have held. It is not the 5× that would have meant
broken construction, and it does not trip §4's kill — but the prediction said "in range" and it is
not in range.

## 8 · What it is, and three things it is not

**It is** the first arm in this programme to clear 30% on a number that does not depend on a start
date. Against A6 read honestly — family mean against family mean, since `w10_t5` *is* the
phase-averaged figure:

| | CAGR | Sharpe | bootstrap DD | trades/wk | 9-yr costs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| SPMO | 21.38% | 0.989 | −31.0% | 0 | 0 |
| A6 family mean | 24.14% | 0.917 | −36.6% | 2.03 | $107k |
| A6 centre | 26.73% | 0.980 | −36.6% | 2.03 | $107k |
| **w10_t5** | **32.13%** | **0.931** | **−49.6%** | **6.55** | **$248k** |

**+7.99 points of CAGR over A6's family mean, at a marginally higher Sharpe** (0.931 against
0.917). Not leverage alone — it is slightly better risk-adjusted *and* materially higher-returning
than A6's honest number. It is still below the ETF risk-adjusted.

**It is not sensitivity-tested.** A6's headline fell 2.59 points the moment its §3 grid ran, and
`w10_t5` has had no grid at all — N, the trail, and the pool have never been perturbed on this
clock. **Its 32.13% should be read the way A6's 26.73% should have been read before the grid: as
the top of a range, not the middle of one.** Having just watched that happen, treating this number
as settled would be the same error twice.

**It is not a five-account blend.** The construction holds ten names once each at 1/10, with pairs
refreshing on different weekly phases. A literal blend of five independent accounts would hold a
persistently top-ranked name at up to 5× weight. This is the object a single account can actually
hold — which is the right test, since Zak has one account — but it is not arithmetically the same
thing as "the mean of five books," and landing within 0.7 points of that mean is therefore an
empirical result rather than a tautology.

**It is not cheap in attention.** 340.7 names/yr is **6.55 trades a week**, against the 2.03 that
ruling 2 cleared. Ruling 2 said turnover is a reporting line and to focus on performance; it did
not contemplate a 3.2× increase, and this is the number most likely to be regretted later. It is
reported here rather than assumed away.

## 9 · Decision line, resolved

The line as pre-registered:

> `w10_t5` clears §4 and beats A6 on both return and Sharpe → it replaces A6 as the live candidate,
> and the plan amendment names it. **Clears the 30% but not the Sharpe → a two-arm decision for
> Zak, explicitly framed as more compounding for more drawdown, which is the trade he has said
> three times he wants.** Fails §4 → A6 stands as the candidate at family-mean expectations, and
> the weekly clock is closed out as a lottery rather than an arm.

**The middle branch is the one we are in**, and it resolves to a decision rather than a
recommendation. The two arms, stated as the trade they are:

| | A6 (banded, family mean) | w10_t5 (weekly, tranched) |
|---|---|---|
| compounding | 24.14% | **32.13%** |
| Sharpe | 0.917 | 0.931 |
| expected drawdown | −36.6% | **−49.6%** |
| effort | 2.03 trades/wk | **6.55 trades/wk** |
| 9-yr costs on $200k | $107k | $248k |
| sensitivity-tested | **yes — and it cost 2.59 pts** | **no** |

At $200k over ten years the compounding gap is not small: 24.14% compounds to ~$1.75m, 32.13% to
~$3.3m. That is the case for the weekly arm, and ruling 1's stated objective — highest compounding,
drawdown accepted — points at it.

Against that: **its number has never been perturbed, and the one arm here that *was* perturbed lost
2.59 points to it.** The honest expectation for `w10_t5` after an equivalent grid is *below* 32.13%,
by an unknown amount. Running that grid is the obvious next step and it is the one thing that would
make this comparison fair. It costs ~7 more cells.

**My recommendation: run the weekly arm's sensitivity grid before choosing.** A6's grid changed
which arm looked better; this one might too, and it is a few hours of compute against a decision
about real money. If Zak wants to move now regardless, the weekly arm is the one that meets the
stated objective — sized in the knowledge that its headline is the untested one.
