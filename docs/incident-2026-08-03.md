# Incident — production database down, disk full (2026-08-03 01:49 UTC)

**Status: RESOLVED 02:12 UTC — the instance completed recovery on its own once the volume had
room. All data intact. Schedules and Routines remain paused pending the re-run.**

## What happened

```
01:49:52  ERROR  cannot execute INSERT in a read-only transaction     <- backfill dies
01:49:53  ERROR  cannot execute UPDATE in a read-only transaction     <- so does report_fail
01:49:56  LOG    database system was not properly shut down; automatic recovery in progress
```

Postgres flipped read-only to protect itself. Then it got worse than read-only: crash recovery
replays the write-ahead log, and finishing that replay needs to WRITE. There was no room.

```
LOG    redo starts at 1/25000028
LOG    redo done at 1/5AFFFF20
FATAL  could not write to file "pg_wal/xlogtemp.NNNNNN": No space left on device
LOG    startup process exited with exit code 1
LOG    shutting down due to startup process failure
```

That loop has repeated every ~25 seconds since. The instance is not degraded — it is **down**, and
it cannot self-heal, because recovery is precisely the thing that needs the space it lacks. The
project API still reports `ACTIVE_HEALTHY`; that describes the control plane, not the database.

## The backfill was the trigger, not the cause

My first reading — "the ten-year backfill filled the disk" — does not survive arithmetic.

The organization is on **pro**, so the disk is 8 GB. The full ten-year `prices` table is nowhere
near that:

| | rows | heap | + PK index | total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| before (3 yr) | 2.21M | ~220 MB | ~130 MB | ~350 MB |
| after (10 yr) | ~7.4M | ~750 MB | ~400 MB | ~1.2 GB |

And the backfill never got close to writing that. It ran for **about 2.5 minutes** (01:47:15 →
01:49:52) with 8 workers against a per-name endpoint. At that rate it had fetched a few hundred
names — on the order of 10⁵–10⁶ rows, perhaps 100–200 MB. **A disk with meaningful free space does
not fill in 2.5 minutes at that write rate.** It was already at the wall; the backfill was simply
the next writer to touch it.

### What actually filled it — measured, once the database answered

I guessed twice and was wrong twice. First I said the bar data filled the disk; the arithmetic
above kills that. Then I blamed the raw filing archive and vacuum bloat. Here is the measurement:

| relation | total on disk |
|---|---|
| `prices` (now 3.73M rows, 10 yr for 923 names) | 592 MB |
| `fundamentals` (2,834 rows incl. every raw document) | 317 MB |
| everything else combined | < 10 MB |
| **whole database** | **927 MB** |
| **`pg_wal` (56 segments), after recovery drained it** | **880 MB** |

No replication slots exist, so nothing was pinning WAL. The archive is not the problem — the
raw-document store is 317 MB, an order of magnitude below my guess.

**The cause is the write-ahead log, exactly as the fatal error said.** `max_wal_size` is **4 GB**
on this instance, which means Postgres is *permitted* to let `pg_wal` grow to several gigabytes
during a sustained write burst before checkpoints force it down. The backfill wrote **1.5 million
rows in about 2.5 minutes** — and because it was an `on conflict do update` upsert scattered across
a 400 MB table, nearly every write touched a fresh page after a checkpoint and logged the whole
8 KB page. That full-page-write amplification is how ~150 MB of row data becomes multiple GB of
WAL. The volume ran out of room, Postgres went read-only, and then recovery could not finish
because finishing also needed to write.

So: not data volume, not the archive, not bloat. **An unthrottled bulk upsert out-running the
checkpointer.** The data was never large; the log was.

## What this cost

- **Yuna is offline.** Every scheduled job and all five sessions are paused (below) rather than
  left to fail on a loop.
- **No expected data loss.** The failed transaction rolls back; `prices` upserts are idempotent, so
  a partial backfill leaves valid rows, not half-rows. **Verify, do not assume.**
- Supabase Pro holds daily physical backups, and `backup` commits a logical dump to the repo. Both
  are fallbacks if recovery ever fails outright.

## How it came back

At 02:12 UTC the instance completed recovery without intervention — the volume gained room (Pro
disk auto-scaling is the likely mechanism), the startup process finished its WAL write, and
checkpoints then drained `pg_wal` from its peak back to 880 MB. Nothing was restored from backup.

**Verified intact after recovery:**

- `prices` 3,730,358 rows, 2016-08-05 → 2026-07-31; 923 names now carry history deeper than the
  old three-year window. The partial backfill left valid rows, not half-rows — as an idempotent
  upsert should.
- `fundamentals` 2,834 rows · `bench` 64 ranked · `book` 7 open positions. Unchanged.
- The `backfill` run row was stuck at `running` (it died before it could mark itself red) and has
  been closed as red with the reason attached.

**Before restoring the schedule — in this order:**

1. **Run `check` first.** It writes nothing but its own report row, which makes it the safest
   possible first contact with a database fresh out of recovery.
2. Finish the backfill with the guard in place — it now skips the 923 names already deepened, so
   the retry is strictly smaller than the attempt that failed.
3. Re-run `score`, then `check` again, and compare the bench against the pre-incident state.
4. Only then restore the schedules (crons are commented out verbatim in `.github/workflows/*.yml`)
   and re-enable the five Routines.

## What this does NOT mean

The archive is fine. `fundamentals` including every raw filing document is 317 MB, and §4.3's
"kept forever" costs far less than I feared while guessing. No plan edit is needed on retention.

## What I got wrong

Three things, in order of seriousness.

1. **I ran an unthrottled bulk rewrite of the largest table against production.** 1.5M upserted
   rows in 2.5 minutes, no pacing, no ceiling, no ability to observe what it was consuming. The
   write rate was the defect; the row count never was.
2. **I sized the wrong budget.** I checked the API cost (~2,762 units against a 100,000/day limit)
   and concluded it was cheap. Cheap in quota, expensive in write throughput — and I never asked
   the second question.
3. **I published two confident diagnoses before I had evidence.** "The backfill filled the disk"
   and then "the raw archive filled the disk" were both wrong, and both were stated to Zak as
   findings rather than guesses. The measurement took one query once the database answered. The
   discipline the plan already demands — cross-check the model's output against an independent
   computation on the raw data — applies to incident diagnosis too, and I skipped it twice under
   time pressure.

## The guard

`db.wal_bytes()` reads the WAL directory; `db.wait_for_wal()` pauses a bulk writer until the
checkpointer catches up, and **stops the job cleanly** if it will not drain — a truncated backfill
is resumable, a database that cannot finish crash recovery is not. `backfill.py` now checks
between batches and skips names already deep enough, so any retry is smaller than the attempt.
Four tests in `tests/test_backfill_guard.py` pin the behaviour, including the one that matters
most: a role that cannot read `pg_ls_waldir` proceeds rather than blocking, because a diagnostic
that can halt production is worse than the thing it diagnoses.
