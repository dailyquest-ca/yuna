# Five arms on the corrected engine — 2026-08-12

The first arms run since the tape fix, the universe reclassification, the duplicate-guard fix and
the accounting assertions. Window 2017-09-01 → 2026-08-07 (2,328 sessions), $200,000 USD start,
identical for every arm. **VOO returned +260.99% over the same window.**

Every run passed conformance, and every run passed the two new accounting invariants — cash
conserved to the cent, and no borrowing. The arithmetic under these numbers is checked.

## 1. Nothing here is close to the benchmark

The best arm compounds at 5.15% a year against VOO's 15.4%. This is not a ranking exercise between
winners; it is a ranking between things that all lose to buying the index, by a wide margin.

## 2. A1's headline is one trade

71.4% of A1's entire profit is **MU, +538% over 239 days**. The top three trades are 96.1% of it,
and the top five are 106.4% — the other 91 trades are net negative. §7i of the 2026-08-10 findings
already reached this from the other side: *"The result is one position, and it is not closed."*
The corrected engine says it more starkly. A1 is not a strategy returning 56%; it is a strategy
that held Micron once.

## 3. C1 has the best mechanics and the worst bleed

C1 wins half its trades with the highest expectancy of the set, on nearly twice A1's sample — the
only thing today that looks like an edge rather than an accident. But read the absolute figures,
because C1's small net inflates its ratios: **A1's top ten make $144k while the rest lose $31k;
C1's top ten make $103k while the rest lose $69k.** C1 wins often and small, then gives most of it
back. That is also why it carries the worst drawdown of the non-levered arms at −22.7%.

## 4. C1 vs C3 isolates the exit, not the screen

Near-identical win rates (49.7% vs 49.2%) and a two-thirds collapse in expectancy (+9.80% → +3.66%).
Same entries, same hit rate, very different payoff. **Whatever the C-family has, it is in the exit
rule.** That is the cheapest place to look next, and it is a narrow target.

## 5. M1 is the finding — positive edge, negative account

M1 is the only arm that takes real size: **41.0% exposure against 8–17% everywhere else.** It has a
**positive per-trade expectancy of +2.21%** and still **loses 8.07%**, with a **−39.89% drawdown**.
A positive edge and a negative account is not a bad strategy; it is over-betting a mediocre one, and
this repeats the pattern already recorded at §7f — *"a real edge by every per-trade measure, and a
−53.5% drawdown."*

Four of the five arms have positive expectancy. Only two beat the baseline. **The gap between
per-trade edge and account return is entirely sizing and exposure**, and it runs the wrong way:
in this set, the arms that stay small survive and the one that deploys blows up.

## What this says about the capital layer

Zak's 2026-08-12 ruling — idle capital into VOO, book capped at 90% deployed — is *strengthened*
by M1, not threatened by it. The lesson of M1 is **not** "the sleeve needs more exposure." It is
that these rules cannot safely carry size in single names. Parking the idle 80%+ in the index is
precisely the way to collect the market return without asking the sleeve to take risk it has now
twice demonstrated it cannot hold.

That reframes the sleeve's job: it does not need to beat VOO on its own capital. It needs to beat
VOO *on the fraction it actually deploys*, while the rest earns the index. On present evidence not
even that is established — A1 aside, and A1 is one name.

## Caveats that bound all six numbers

- **`law_stamp` cannot separate rule surfaces** (open). Every arm here shares the stamp `2026-08-09`
  with runs that had a materially different hypothesis surface, so cross-run attribution to a
  specific ruleset is not provable from the record.
- **Costs are applied but unsourced.** 5bps deep / 15bps thin half-spread, zero commission, zero FX
  (a recorded ruling). The spread figures trace to no document. If they flatter, they flatter every
  arm equally — the ranking survives, the absolute level may not.
- **Fundamentals are point-in-time on `reportDate`** (verified), but read from the *latest* vendor
  document, so a restated EPS reads as restated rather than as originally printed.
- **A1's 96 trades and C1's 175 are small samples** for a claim about a decade.
