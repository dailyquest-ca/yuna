# WO-A10 · the regime clause

**Zak's ruling, 2026-08-14:** *"When there are bear market or crash indicators we can change our
strategy at that time to be defensive until the market has proof of recovery, and we would stay
S&P or cash. Analyze the crashes carefully and see if you could call out a way this strategy
would work with the notrail strat."*

This is the answer, and it is the strongest single result the A-series has produced. It also
refutes two of my own design hypotheses, which is recorded below rather than quietly dropped.

---

## 1. What the crashes actually did

The measurement that started this, on the survivorship-corrected 2007-2013 deep test:

| SPY vs its own 200-day | sessions | `w5_notrail` returned | annualised |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| above | 1,228 | **+272.4%** | +31.8% |
| below | 531 | **−87.9%** | −79.9% |

Those two compound to the −54.8% that killed the arm over that window. **The strategy is not
broken. It is fully invested through the 30% of sessions in which it loses 80% annualised.**

That is not a subtle edge to be mined. It is the largest single fact about this arm.

---

## 2. The cost that had to be priced first

A 200-day gate is not free, and §4.4 had already rejected a one-touch version of it for costing
6.2 CAGR points. The reason is whipsaw: SPY made **28 separate trips below its 200-day in
2006-2013 and 32 in 2014-2026** — one true signal per era and roughly thirty false ones. A
one-touch gate pays a round trip for every one of them.

So the design hypothesis was a **latch**: short confirmation to go defensive, long confirmation to
come back, plus a `rising` clause as the "proof of recovery" Zak asked for. Cheap to exit, slow to
re-enter.

**Both halves of that hypothesis are wrong.** They are wrong in a specific and useful way.

---

## 3. Refutation one — asymmetry made the crash window monotonically worse

Crash window, 2007-01-05 → 2013-12-31, 1,760 sessions, park SPY, $200k start:

| cell | latch (out, in) | rising | end NAV | CAGR | max DD |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `w5_g_1_10` | 1, 10 | — | $275,031 | **+4.68%** | −60.7% |
| `w5_g_plain` | 1, 1 | — | $255,918 | +3.60% | −62.9% |
| `w5_g_3_20r` | 3, 20 | yes | $239,049 | +2.60% | −67.9% |
| `w5_g_3_20` | 3, 20 | — | $222,811 | +1.57% | −67.9% |
| `w5_g_5_40r` | 5, 40 | yes | $201,331 | +0.11% | −69.1% |
| `w5_notrail` | ungated | — | $90,298 | **−10.75%** | −82.5% |

More asymmetry is monotonically worse. The reason is 2009: the recovery was violent, and a long
re-entry confirmation simply missed it. `rising` is worth +1.03 points at (3,20) on its own — the
recovery-proof idea is real — but it cannot pay for the delay it is bundled with.

## 4. Refutation two — the latch is a spike, not a plateau, and the *unlatched* gate wins

I flagged before running this that `(1,10)` topping a five-cell sweep was suspect, because it was
also the **shortest** `confirm_in` anyone had tried — "best" and "smallest asymmetry tested" were
the same cell. This programme has been burned by that twice. So the ladder was filled in.

Benign window, 2017-08-15 → 2026-08-13, 2,261 sessions, park SPMO, calendar VOO:

| cell | latch | CAGR | vs ungated | max DD |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `w5_notrail` | ungated | **44.79%** | — | **−48.7%** |
| `w5_g_1_7` | 1, 7 | 40.85% | −3.94 | −50.8% |
| `w5_g_1_3` | 1, 3 | 40.45% | −4.34 | −52.7% |
| `w5_g_1_10` | 1, 10 | 38.34% | −6.45 | −53.3% |
| `w5_g_1_5` | 1, 5 | 38.23% | −6.56 | −51.4% |
| `w5_g_1_10r` | 1, 10 + rising | 35.48% | −9.31 | −53.1% |
| `w5_g_plain` | 1, 1 | 35.34% | −9.45 | −56.1% |
| `w5_g_3_20` | 3, 20 | 34.03% | −10.76 | −54.5% |
| `w5_g_1_14` | 1, 14 | 32.61% | −12.18 | −57.4% |
| `w5_g_1_20` | 1, 20 | 31.40% | −13.39 | −54.1% |
| `w5_g_3_20r` | 3, 20 + rising | 28.60% | −16.19 | −58.6% |
| `w5_g_5_40r` | 5, 40 + rising | 23.09% | −21.70 | −53.9% |

Two things to read here, and the second is the one that matters.

**The ladder is noise.** 3 → −4.34, 5 → −6.56, 7 → −3.94, 10 → −6.45, 14 → −12.18, 20 → −13.39.
`(1,10)`'s immediate neighbours differ from it by up to 8 points in both directions. It is a local
spike on one window, exactly as suspected. **No latch setting has earned adoption.**

**Every gate deepens the drawdown on this window.** Ungated −48.7%; every gated cell between −50.8%
and −58.6%. A defensive overlay that makes the worst drawdown worse is not defensive here — it
exits after the fall has started and re-enters after the snapback has started, which in a V-shaped
selloff (2018Q4, 2020, 2022) locks the loss and misses the repair.

On this window alone the honest verdict is: **the gate is a cost with no compensating risk
benefit.** That is what §4.4 concluded, and on 2017-2026 it was right.

## 5. The window split was the wrong instrument — the continuous curve settles it

Both tables above are windows. Splicing them is arithmetic on two different parks over a period
with a three-year hole in it. Since the backfill now reaches 2005 and SPY prints from 2003, the
whole thing can be run as **one uninterrupted equity curve**, which removes the splice and the
park mismatch together.

Full cycle, 2007-01-05 → 2026-08-13, **4,932 sessions**, park SPY throughout, $200k start:

| cell | latch | end NAV | CAGR | max DD | Sharpe | bootstrap p5 | skew |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `w5_g_plain` | 1, 1 | **$8,932,676** | **21.39%** | −62.9% | **0.672** | **+5.6%** | +0.34 |
| `w5_g_1_10` | 1, 10 | $7,266,830 | 20.12% | −60.7% | 0.657 | +4.5% | +0.32 |
| `w5_g_1_5` | 1, 5 | $6,964,263 | 19.86% | −61.1% | 0.650 | +4.2% | +0.33 |
| `w5_g_1_7` | 1, 7 | $6,926,229 | 19.83% | −60.6% | 0.650 | +4.1% | +0.31 |
| `w5_g_1_10r` | 1, 10 + rising | $6,031,310 | 18.98% | −60.7% | 0.634 | +3.8% | +0.30 |
| `w5_g_1_3` | 1, 3 | $5,697,809 | 18.64% | −62.2% | 0.623 | +3.1% | +0.35 |
| `w5_g_1_14` | 1, 14 | $4,382,509 | 17.06% | −60.4% | 0.595 | +2.4% | +0.23 |
| `w5_g_1_20` | 1, 20 | $3,734,547 | 16.11% | **−60.2%** | 0.574 | +1.9% | +0.30 |
| `w5_notrail` | ungated | $3,103,088 | 15.02% | **−82.5%** | 0.535 | **−3.4%** | +0.14 |
| SPY | — | — | 11.15% | — | — | — | — |

*(`w5_g_3_20`, `w5_g_3_20r` and `w5_g_5_40r` were still running when this was written; they were
last on both prior windows and the ladder below is monotone into them, but they are not reported
here until they land.)*

On the full cycle the `confirm_in` ladder is **monotone in the opposite direction to the design**:
1 → 21.39, 5 → 19.86, 7 → 19.83, 10 → 20.12, 14 → 17.06, 20 → 16.11. Every session of re-entry
delay costs money, and the best cell is the one that waits zero. (The 3 → 18.64 rung is the one
break in it, and at 1.2 points below its neighbours it is the same noise §4 found on the benign
window — which is precisely why no rung of this ladder should be adopted.)

**Every gated cell dominates the ungated arm on every axis at once** — return, drawdown, Sharpe,
bootstrap floor and skew. That is the finding. The *latch parameter* surface is noise, but the
*gate / no-gate* decision is not, and the winner is the cell with **no free parameters at all**:
plain `regime_ok`, one touch either way, nothing tuned.

The number I would put first is not the CAGR. It is the **bootstrap 5th percentile**: block
bootstrap, 63-session blocks, 10,000 draws, seed 0.

- ungated: **−3.4%/yr** — in the worst 5% of resampled paths this arm *loses money over 20 years*
- gated: **+5.6%/yr**

That is the answer to "how could we make the Sharpe more stable." Not a smoother stop, not a
volatility target — a regime clause. Sharpe 0.535 → 0.672, skew +0.14 → +0.34, max drawdown
−82.5% → −62.9%.

## 6. Where the edge comes from — it is not SPY

The obvious objection: the gated cells sit in SPY for a fifth of the run, and SPY compounded at
11.15% over this window. Is the gate just a closet index fund?

No. Decomposing `w5_g_plain`'s own equity curve by whether the book was parked that session:

| | sessions | share | compounded | annualised |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| invested | 3,902 | 79.1% | +3,770.0% | **+26.6%** |
| parked in SPY | 1,029 | 20.9% | +15.5% | **+3.6%** |

The park earned **+15.5% in total over roughly four years of sessions**. It contributes something
like 0.7 points a year to the 21.39%. The edge is the *avoidance*, not the parking vehicle: the
same strategy compounds at **26.6%/yr when the gate says invest** against 15.0%/yr when it is
always invested.

**This directly answers the "S&P or cash" half of Zak's ruling.** The choice between them is worth
well under a point a year, because the parked windows are precisely the windows in which SPY does
nothing. Park in whichever is operationally simpler. The gate is worth 6.4 points; the vehicle is
worth a rounding error.

**The clause does nothing in eight of twenty years.** It rescues six years by 8 to 32 points and
costs in four by 2 to 21. That asymmetry is the whole result.

Two rows deserve comment.

**2009 is the momentum crash.** The gate was defensive for 42% of that year and *still* beat the
ungated arm by 31.5 points. The ungated arm was destroying capital in 2009 while the index
recovered, because the momentum leaders going into March 2009 were the previous year's survivors
and they were annihilated in the reversal. This is the exact event Barroso–Santa-Clara is about,
and it is the event the sleeve governor was built for. A 200-day index gate handles it.

**2022 is the whipsaw, visible and priced.** Defensive 73% of the year, and it ended 2.2 points
*behind*. That is what the false signals cost when there is no crash to catch.

## 8. What I got wrong

Recorded because the pattern matters more than the individual errors.

1. **I designed for asymmetry and asymmetry lost.** Every step away from the plain one-touch gate
   made the crash window worse, and the full cycle agreed. The reasoning — "whipsaw is the cost,
   so make re-entry expensive" — was sound and the measurement disagreed. Whipsaw is real but the
   *recovery* is where the money is, and delaying re-entry costs more than the whipsaw it avoids.
2. **I nearly recommended `(1,10)` off a five-cell sweep.** It topped that sweep because it was the
   shortest setting tried, not because it is good. The bracket shows its neighbours swinging 8
   points around it. Had the ladder not been filled in, a spike would have gone into the plan.
3. **I priced this on two spliced windows when one continuous run was available.** The splice put
   `w5_notrail` at ~17.2%/yr; the continuous curve says 15.0%. The splice was optimistic because it
   silently omitted 2014-2016. The data to do it properly had been in the database since the
   backfill finished.

---

## 9. What this does not settle

- **The gate is measured on one index rule.** `regime_ok` is close > 200-day SMA on SPY. That rule
  was not tuned, which is its main defence, but neither was it compared against alternatives
  (10-month, 12-1 index momentum, breadth). Untested, not endorsed.
- **The recent decade says the opposite.** On 2017-2026 the gate costs 9.45 points and deepens the
  drawdown. The full-cycle case rests on 2008, 2009, 2011, 2015 and 2016. Anyone who believes the
  next decade looks like the last one should not want this clause.
- **`w5_notrail` itself is not settled.** WO-A9 §4's execution-asymmetry finding stands: removing
  the stop removed the only conservatively-filled exits in the model.
- **Park in SPY is a stand-in.** The 20-year run parks in SPY because SPMO's first bar is
  2015-10-12. Zak's ruling puts unused cash in SPMO. §6 shows the vehicle is worth under a point a
  year, so this does not change the verdict, but the stored ten-year cells and the 20-year cells
  are not park-comparable and must not be quoted side by side.
- **Nothing here is a plan amendment.** §3.2 has no regime clause. Adopting one is a plan edit
  first and a code change second, and this document is the evidence for that edit, not the edit.

---

## 10. What I would recommend

Adopt the **plain gate** — `regime_ok` on SPY's 200-day, one touch each way, no latch, no rising
clause — as a §3.2 amendment to the concentrated arm, parked per Zak's existing ruling in SPMO.

It is the cell with no fitted parameters, it dominates the ungated arm on every risk and return
measure over the full cycle, and its single largest contribution is turning a strategy whose worst
5% of paths lose money into one whose worst 5% still compound at 5.6%.

The thing to weigh against it is §9's second bullet, and it is not small: this clause would have
cost roughly a fifth of the last decade's return.
